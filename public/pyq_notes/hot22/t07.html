The 1991 crisis and the LPG reforms

Asked in every 2025–26 exam (Phase 13, Steno 2025, CGL 2025, CHSL 2025, Steno 2026, Phase-XIV) · most asked: why the reforms came (BoP crisis), IMF + World Bank, LPG meaning, disinvestment, Narasimham / Chelliah, LERMS and convertibility

Why 1991? The crisis

In 1991 India almost ran out of foreign money. This was a Balance of Payments (BoP) crisis PYQ ×3 — the country could not pay for imports or repay its foreign borrowings PYQ ×2. Reserves fell so low they were not enough even for a fortnight (about 2 weeks) of imports. PYQ

CauseWhat it means
BoP crisisFar more dollars going out than coming in; foreign-exchange reserves fell sharply
High fiscal deficitGovt spending far above its income (about 8.4% of GDP in 1990–91) CGL+
InflationPrices rising fast
External debt burdenBig foreign loans and interest to repay PYQ (answer: crisis "relating to its external debt")
Gulf War (1990–91)Oil prices shot up, so the import bill rose
Weak exportsExports were too small to pay for imports
Political/economic instabilityFrequent changes of government in 1989–91

How India handled it

  • Took loans and help from the IMF and the World Bank PYQ ×5. The help came with conditions — a Structural Adjustment Programme (cut the deficit, devalue, open trade). PYQ One PYQ called the World Bank by its formal name, International Bank for Reconstruction and Development. PYQ
  • Gold reserves were used as security (collateral) to raise foreign loans — about 67 tonnes were pledged. CGL+
  • Immediate step: devaluation of the rupee PYQ — done in July 1991 (see below).
  • Short-term aim of these stabilisation steps: control inflation and restore the BoP PYQ; fiscal deficit to come down to about 3–4% of GDP over the medium term. PYQ The long-term changes (LPG) are called structural reforms; privatisation of PSUs is a structural reform. PYQ

LPG: the three words

LetterWordMeaning in one line
LLiberalisationCut government controls and restrictions inside the economy; aim: open up various sectors PYQ
PPrivatisation PYQRaise the role / ownership of the private sector
GGlobalisation PYQLink the Indian economy more closely with the world economy
  • Along with LPG came: deregulation, de-licensing, disinvestment, FDI liberalisation, foreign technology, trade reforms, tax reforms, financial-sector reforms and exchange-rate reforms.
  • "Not a component of 1991 reforms?" → Socialisation (it is the opposite idea). PYQ
  • FDI belongs to the Globalisation pillar. PYQ FDI + international trade + foreign technology = the push towards globalisation.
  • Globalisation = integrating India with the global market by cutting tariffs, giving export incentives and inviting FDI. PYQ
  • "Which is NOT a liberalisation measure?" → Privatisation of PSUs (that is P, not L). PYQ But selling govt shares in PSUs = a tool of Privatisation. PYQ
Trick: L = "Lagaam dheeli" (loosen the reins at home), P = "Private ko pass do", G = "Global se jodo". FDI, trade, foreign tech → all G.

Liberalisation: the end of the Licence Raj

Before 1991, licences, permits, quotas and controls decided who could start or expand a factory. This was the Licence Raj (Licence-Permit-Quota Raj) PYQ ×3 — the industrial licensing system. PYQ Its side-effects: barriers to entry for new firms, hindered expansion, inefficiency PYQ ×2; big firms could even block rivals by hoarding licences PYQ; protected firms had little reason to upgrade technology. PYQ Licensing did not promote innovation in small industry — it created rent-seeking (A false, R true). PYQ

  • Aim of liberalisation: cut licences, permits, controls, restrictions and regulations → more efficiency, competition, investment, productivity. One aim was to remove barriers to entry and growth of firms (A–R: both true, R explains A). PYQ
  • The biggest step of the 1991 Industrial Policy: industrial licensing abolished for most industries = de-licensing. Abolition of licensing in all but a few industries PYQ ×2. In 1991 only 18 industries stayed under licensing. CGL+
  • Modern trend for business licences: make them easier to get. PYQ
SSC trap"In 1991 all industries were de-licensed" — false. Most were; a few strategic, hazardous or sensitive ones stayed under licensing. Today licensing remains for items like alcoholic drinks, tobacco products (cigars, cigarettes), defence and aerospace electronics, industrial explosives and hazardous chemicals. PYQ CGL+

Privatisation: public sector, disinvestment, PSU "ratnas"

  • 1991 limited the public-sector monopoly, opened more fields to private firms, pushed restructuring of sick or loss-making PSUs, and took the disinvestment policy forward.
  • Industries reserved for the public sector cut from 17 to 8; later cut further (today only 2: atomic energy and railway operations). CGL+
  • Why privatise? To cut the burden of loss-making PSUs and improve efficiency through competition — a shift in the role of the state from owner to facilitator. PYQ ×2 A–R: NEP 1991 made markets more efficient; privatisation cut govt control and raised productivity — both true, R explains A. PYQ
  • "Minimum Government, Maximum Governance" = a leaner state that facilitates rather than controls. PYQ
DisinvestmentPrivatisation
MeaningGovt sells part of its shares (equity) in a PSU to the public or private investors PYQ ×5Private sector's ownership or control goes up
ControlGovt may still keep control (e.g. a minority sale) PYQControl can pass to the private owner (strategic sale)
BudgetMoney from it is a capital receipt, not revenue PYQ ×2—

Trick: Every disinvestment ≠ full privatisation. "Kuch share becho, control rakho" = disinvestment. Disinvestment is not a revenue receipt — selling an asset is capital.
  • Disinvestment has been criticised (selling family silver, undervaluation) — but not because it raised PSU monopoly (A true, R false). PYQ
  • Navratna / Maharatna / Miniratna: status given to well-performing PSUs. They are high-performing PSUs given greater financial and operational autonomy PYQ ×3; under the reforms they were kept in the public sector PYQ. Other status besides Navratna: Miniratna. PYQ Navratna and Miniratna began in 1997, Maharatna in 2009–10. CGL+ Example: ONGC ran oil and gas projects in 16 countries after 1991. PYQ

Private sector, MRTP and foreign investment

  • After 1991, for private firms: entry barriers ↓, licensing ↓, limits on expansion ↓ → competition ↑, private investment ↑.
  • MRTP Act = Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act. Aims: stop concentration of economic power, regulate monopolies, control restrictive trade practices. It was passed in 1969 — during the Fourth Five-Year Plan. PYQ
  • 1991: many MRTP rules needing prior govt approval for big firms to expand were relaxed — focus shifted from control → competition.
SSC trapThe MRTP Act was not abolished in 1991. It was replaced later by the Competition Act, 2002 (Competition Commission of India, 2003; MRTP repealed 2009). CGL+
  • FDI = Foreign Direct Investment. 1991 liberalised it. Benefits: capital, technology, management expertise, productivity, international competitiveness. Main aim: bridge the savings–investment gap and bring in technology and managerial know-how. PYQ
  • Automatic approval up to 51% foreign equity in high-priority industries (34 of them). FDI reforms PYQ ×2 (old FERA-era cap was 40%).
  • A special board, the Foreign Investment Promotion Board (FIPB), was set up after the Industrial Policy of 1991. PYQ CGL+ FIPB was abolished in 2017.
  • A–R: the reforms opened India to foreign investors (true) "because the govt wanted to raise domestic savings rapidly" (false). PYQ
  • Foreign technology agreements made easier. Aim: technology upgradation → modern technology, productivity ↑, quality ↑, export competitiveness ↑.
  • FERA, 1973 treated foreign exchange as scarce and made violations criminal; it did not fit the reforms and was replaced by FEMA, 1999 (civil law). PYQ

  • Pre-1991 strategy = import substitution: make at home what was imported, to protect infant domestic industries. PYQ ×6 Also called the inward-looking trade strategy. PYQ Main criticism: a protected market that bred inefficiency and little innovation. PYQ
  • Late 1980s (Rajiv Gandhi years): selective export promotion and partial liberalisation had already begun. PYQ
  • Statement trap: "trade reform increased import licensing" — false. PYQ Capital goods became cheaper to import as tariffs fell and licensing went. PYQ
  • DGFT (Directorate General of Foreign Trade) was set up in 1991. PYQ Joining the WTO in 1995 deepened India's integration into world trade. PYQ

Tax, banking and fiscal reforms + the committees

  • Tax reforms — aims: rationalise (simplify, lower) tax rates, broaden the tax base, improve compliance, reduce distortions. Committee: Raja J. Chelliah (Tax Reforms Committee, 1991) PYQ ×2 — fewer and lower rates, wider base; foundation of modern tax policy. PYQ
  • Later: Vijay Kelkar task force (2002–03) on direct and indirect taxes PYQ; VAT (2005) and then GST (1 July 2017) improved tax efficiency and base PYQ ×2 ("One Nation, One Tax").
  • Financial-sector reforms — Narasimham Committee, 1991 (chair M. Narasimham) PYQ ×2. Recommended: reduce SLR and CRR PYQ, capital adequacy norms, prudential accounting norms, bank autonomy, a stronger banking system, less directed credit, more competition.
  • Results of banking reform: prudential norms and CRAR PYQ; Board for Financial Supervision (BFS) under RBI (1994) PYQ; new private and foreign banks entered banking PYQ; foreign investment limit in private banks raised to 74% PYQ ×2; NSE created for transparent stock trading PYQ.
  • Narasimham Committee II (1998): merge and consolidate public sector banks. PYQ
  • Malhotra Committee (insurance reform, R. N. Malhotra): report in 1994. PYQ
  • Fiscal reforms — aim: control the fiscal deficit. Measures: control expenditure, tax reforms, rationalise subsidies, better fiscal management. Later: FRBM Act (2003) for fiscal discipline PYQ ×2; under it the Finance Minister places fiscal policy statements before Parliament PYQ; states too had to meet fiscal targets PYQ.
  • Rangarajan Committee (1993) → disinvestment of PSUs. CGL+ (A different Rangarajan expert group, set up in 2012 by the Planning Commission, reviewed poverty measurement. PYQ)
Super trick: Narasimham → Bank ("Nara-Bank"); Chelliah → Tax ("Chela tax bharta hai"); Rangarajan → Disinvestment ("Rang badla: sarkari se private"); Malhotra → insurance ("maal ka bima").

After the reforms: what happened (asked as statements)

  • Sector that gained the most from LPG and drove GDP growth: services. PYQ ×2 India became an outsourcing hub PYQ thanks to low wages and skilled manpower. PYQ
  • Agriculture suffered — cut in fertiliser subsidies PYQ, less public investment and a shift to export cash crops. PYQ
  • Jobless growth: growth led by capital-intensive industry and services did not create enough jobs. PYQ
  • Forex reserves later reached historic highs. PYQ
  • Second-generation reforms (late 1990s onward) aimed to deepen structural change — governance, labour laws, financial regulation. PYQ
  • Pre-1991 slow growth of about 3.5% a year = the "Hindu rate of growth", a term coined by Raj Krishna. PYQ

Economy during the planning era (1950–90)

  • Share of industry in GDP in 1950–51: about 13% PYQ; it rose to about 25% by 1990 — the sector with the largest rise in GDP share PYQ.
  • Structural change failed in farming: about 65% of people still depended on agriculture in 1990. PYQ
  • In the 1950s–60s India imported food grains and machinery (PL-480 wheat) because of shortages. PYQ
  • Public sector was given a dominant role as a tool for social goals and balanced regional development PYQ; but PSUs suffered from bureaucratic and political interference. PYQ
  • Self-reliance aim: reduce dependence on foreign technology, capital and imports by building domestic industry. PYQ
  • Karve Committee (1955) promoted small-scale and village industries for rural development. PYQ Antyodaya (1977) aimed to lift the poorest families. PYQ

After the plans

  • 12th Plan (2012–17) = last Five Year Plan. Planning Commission ended in 2014; NITI Aayog from 1 January 2015.
  • NITI replaced plans with a 15-year vision, 7-year strategy and 3-year action agenda. CGL+

IPR 1956 — the "economic constitution" of India

The Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 followed the Avadi Congress goal of a socialistic pattern of society (1955) CGL+. Its central theme: state control of the commanding heights PYQ; key feature: expansion of the public sector PYQ ×2; main objective (PYQ answer): growth with equity. PYQ It divided industries into three schedules, with the public sector leading in strategic and heavy industries. PYQ ×4

ScheduleWho runs itDetail
AState only17 industries, exclusive responsibility of the State (arms, atomic energy, iron and steel, railways, air transport…)
BState + private12 industries CGL+; State would set up new units more and more; private firms could also work
CMainly privateAll the rest; open to private sector, under government regulation / licensing
  • Statement "IPR 1956 aimed at private-sector dominance in all key industries" — false; "it classified industries into three schedules" — true. PYQ
  • Why licensing even to expand an existing private factory? To keep production under government control (output and investment in line with plan targets). PYQ
Trick: A = Apni sarkar (state only), B = Both, C = Companies (private). And 1956 = Mahalanobis + Public sector + Socialistic pattern ("MPS").

The United Nations

  • UN: founded 1945 (Charter in force 24 October 1945 — UN Day 24 Oct); HQ New York; 193 members; 6 official languages (Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, Spanish CGL+). India is a founding member.
  • 6 principal organs: General Assembly (UNGA), Security Council (UNSC), Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC), International Court of Justice (ICJ), Secretariat, Trusteeship Council (inactive since 1994 CGL+).
  • UNSC: 15 members PYQ — 5 permanent (USA, UK, France, Russia, China) with veto, and 10 non-permanent (2-year terms). BRICS members that are P5: China and Russia. PYQ
  • ICJ: 1945; seat at The Hague, Netherlands PYQ; 15 judges, 9-year terms; the UN's principal judicial organ. It is the only principal organ not in New York.
  • UN declared 2025 the International Year of Quantum Science and Technology. PYQ First woman Permanent Representative of India to the UN: Ruchira Kamboj. PYQ

Bretton Woods twins

The IMF and the World Bank were planned at the Bretton Woods Conference (New Hampshire, USA, July 1944) — hence "Bretton Woods institutions". Both are in Washington, D.C. In 1991 India turned to both of them for loans. PYQ
